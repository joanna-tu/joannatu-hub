Stall: cannot get it through the organisation

Have the champion say the case back in their own words

Write one line on what the buyer is trying to achieve, then have the champion say it back without your document. Where their version differs from yours is the gap to work on.

Evidence: documented practice. Sellers and trainers publish how they do it. No study has tested it.

What it is.

Your champion nods through every call. You have never heard them explain the deal without you.

Write down, in one short statement, what you believe the buyer is trying to achieve and why. Then ask your champion to say it back in their own words, and do not correct them while they talk. Where their version differs from yours is a gap that will show up inside their organisation, and you only see it when the champion speaks instead of nodding.

The harder version is to have the champion deliver the statement to their own executive while you are there, or rehearse it with you before a meeting you will not attend. The test is the same: can the person who has to carry your case state it with your document shut? The military calls this a backbrief: before they go and carry out an order, the people below brief back how they intend to do it.

What it looks like.

Stephen Bungay's briefing template, from The Art of Action (2010), reproduced in chapter summaries Bungay endorsed. One page in six sections: the context; the intent one and two levels up; your own intent, split into what, in order to, why and how it will be measured; the tasks that follow, with owners and timing; the freedoms and constraints; and last, a backbrief that asks whether the situation has changed, with only three allowed answers. The brief still holds. The tasks change but the aim survives. Or the aim itself has to change.

The Army's running order, from a 1998 Center for Army Lessons Learned newsletter. Straight after an order, each unit gives a confirmation brief from a four-point chart, in this order: what the enemy will most probably do, the higher commander's intent, the decisive points, and the unit's own task and purpose. All the confirmation briefs together are capped at fifteen minutes. The backbrief comes later, once the unit has worked out how it will do the job, and each one is capped at ten minutes. The Army's field manual FM 6-0 defines the backbrief as subordinates briefing the commander on how they intend to accomplish their mission.

In sales the wording is not published. The nearest thing is Elay Cohen's seventh test of a champion at SalesHood, from January 2024: ask the champion to explain how your solution differs from the competitors and why they think yours fits best, because the decision team will ask them when you are not there. That tests the differences, not the buyer's intent.

Where it has been tested.

In B2B sales

Nobody has measured it. No study compares deals where the champion restated the intent with deals where they did not. SalesHood, which sells sales enablement software, publishes the clearest practitioner version. The nearest B2B finding is about the room, not the move: Karl Schmidt, Brent Adamson and Anna Bird reported from CEB's research in Harvard Business Review in 2015 that buying authority now sits with a group whose members can each block the deal.

In other disciplines

Military doctrine, also unmeasured. The backbrief is settled practice in Army doctrine, and nobody has compared units that backbriefed with units that did not.

Teach-back in medicine. Jason Talevski and colleagues' 2020 review covered twenty studies of teach-back, nine of them randomised trials: the clinician explains something, the patient explains it back, and the clinician clears up whatever came back wrong and checks again. Nineteen of the twenty reported improvements, in things like communication, health knowledge and, in both heart failure studies, slightly fewer readmissions. The review rated four of the studies high quality, nine moderate and seven weak.

Saying it beats rereading it. Henry Roediger and Jeffrey Karpicke had students read prose passages and then either read them again or write down what they remembered, published in 2006. Five minutes later the rereaders did slightly better, 81% against 75%. A week later the students who had recalled the text held 56% against 42%. In a second experiment, students who recalled three times held 61% after a week against 40% for students who only reread, even though the rereaders had seen the text far more often.

Caveat.

The teach-back trials are one clinician with one patient, not a champion carrying an argument into a meeting you never see. The recall experiments used students, prose passages and nothing at stake. Nobody has compared deals where the champion restated the intent with deals where they did not.

Takeaway.

Write one line on what the buyer is trying to achieve and why, have the champion say it back with your document shut, and listen for what they drop. Outside selling, explaining a message back is the best-tested check that it was understood, and producing an argument sticks better than hearing one. Inside selling nothing has been measured, so count on what the gap tells you, not on the move closing anything.
