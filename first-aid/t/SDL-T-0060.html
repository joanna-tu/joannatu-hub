Stall: cannot get it through the organisation

Agree how they will decide before you negotiate terms

Before you talk terms, get the buyer to walk you through who approves, in what order, on which dates, and who signs. Then write for each approver, not only for your contact.

Evidence: documented practice. Sellers and trainers publish how they do it. No study has tested it.

What it is.

The proposal went out two weeks ago. Your contact says it is "with the team", and has nothing else to tell you.

Late silence after a proposal can be an approval you cannot see: the proposal is sitting with an owner, a finance lead or a legal team, and your contact has nothing to report, so reports nothing. The fix starts much earlier. Before you discuss price, scope or contract wording, get the buyer to describe how a decision like this actually gets made: which reviews happen and in what order, who has to approve, which committee meets on which date, who signs, and what would slow it all down. Then agree how the two of you will work through that path, and write for the people on it, not only for your contact.

Deepak Malhotra, who teaches negotiation at Harvard Business School, calls this negotiating the process before the substance. His warning: you hold a costly concession back for months, spend it to get the deal over the line, and your counterpart thanks you for the flexibility and says he will share it with his boss. You did not know there was a boss, the negotiation is not over, and you have nothing left to give.

What it looks like.

Malhotra's process questions, from Harvard Business Review in December 2015. Ask how much time their company needs to close the deal, who has to be on board, what might slow the process down or speed it up, and whether there are milestones or dates to know about. Then the small ones: who will be in tomorrow's meeting, what the agenda is, and when the issues that matter to you will be discussed if not then. He adds that you will not always get a clear answer at the start and that sometimes it is too early to ask, so agree as much of the process as you can, as early as is sensible.

Prospeo's two questions. Ask who else needs to sign off, and what their approval process looks like. Then check who is actually authorised to sign before you send the contract, because it is often not your contact. Prospeo sells sales-data software.

The sales methodology names the steps, not the words. MEDDICC splits the buyer's Decision Process into technical validation and business approval, and keeps the Paper Process, how a decision becomes a signature, as a separate thing to map, as taught in Andy Whyte's book. The Go Live Plan template sits inside paid material. None of it publishes the questions to ask.

Where it has been tested.

In B2B sales

Nobody has measured it. No study compares sellers who pinned down the approval path before discussing terms with sellers who did it the other way round.

Legal and finance in the deal are a good sign. Dan Morgese of Gong reported in 2023, from over a million emails and nearly 30,000 calls, that deals where legal was involved in the late stages won 2.6 times as often as deals where it was not. Gong sells the software that produced the figure, and it is a correlation. Read it as a check: if nobody in legal or finance has touched a deal you believe is closing, the buying process you think is running may not be.

One Gong number points the other way. Gong's collection of sales statistics reports win rates 10% higher when sellers discuss pricing on the first call, with no sample or method published. It is about price rather than the approval path, but it does not support holding every term back until the process is agreed.

In other disciplines

Negotiation advice, not a study. Malhotra draws his rule from advising companies on large deals, and says so. Nobody has compared negotiators who settled process first with negotiators who went straight to terms. The experiments on planning backwards from a deadline, which make the date people give close to true, are under The mutual action plan.

Approvals are tiers of people who lack the detail. Henry Mintzberg, Duru Raisinghani and André Théorêt tracked 25 strategic decisions through organisations for their 1976 paper. Proposals that were modified along the way passed through an average of 6.4 selection steps, more than half of them approvals rather than evaluations. The people approving had little time and lacked the knowledge of those who built the proposal, and the sponsor bringing it to them was, in the authors' words, inherently biased. What reaches each tier has to stand on its own, without your contact in the room to explain it.

Caveat.

Nobody has tested whether asking these questions changes the outcome, in selling or anywhere else. The Gong figures are a vendor's correlations, and the organisational research observed decisions rather than changing them. What the move reliably gives you is information about the path, not a better result.

Takeaway.

Before you concede anything, ask who else has to say yes, in what order, on which dates, and who signs, and confirm who will sign before the contract goes out. Then write a page each approver can read without your contact explaining it. If legal and finance have not appeared in a deal you think is closing, treat that as a warning, not a detail.
