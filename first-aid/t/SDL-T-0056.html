Stall: cannot get it through the organisation

Rebuild the case for a cautious committee

When the committee stalls a deal your champion won, rebuild the case for cautious buyers: references from their own industry, everything needed to make it work, and proof from clearly independent sources.

Evidence: assembled by the library. Nobody publishes this as one move. The library put it together from parts that are published.

What it is.

Your champion loved it and carried it through the evaluation. Then the committee went quiet.

The deal may not have lost its argument. It may have changed reader. The champion wanted what the product makes possible; the committee wants to know nothing will go wrong on their watch. So put away the case that won the champion and build the one a cautious group needs: companies in their own industry already running this, everything that has to be in place before it works listed and covered, and a clear answer on who is there when it breaks.

Bring the proof from sources that are independent of each other, not three versions of your own pitch, and do not drip-feed it a reference call a month. People discount several voices once they look like one voice, and persuasion fades fast once it stops arriving.

The two kinds of buyer come from Geoffrey Moore, who split technology buyers into visionaries and pragmatists in Crossing the Chasm. He wrote about positioning a product to a market, not about rescuing one account, so the deal-level version is the library's own step.

What it looks like.

What a pragmatist is buying, in Moore's words. In Crossing the Chasm he writes that pragmatists care about "the company they are buying from", the quality of the product, "the infrastructure of supporting products and system interfaces", and the reliability of the service. Only one of the four is the product. He also writes that pragmatists are "vertically" oriented: they talk more with others like themselves in their own industry. That sentence is the whole of the same-industry rule.

Why the champion's enthusiasm counts against you. In Inside the Tornado Moore writes that "visionaries think pragmatists are pedestrian, and pragmatists think visionaries are dangerous", so visionaries "do not make good references for pragmatists".

Moore's two templates. Crossing the Chasm carries a fill-in positioning statement, whose slots include "our product is a (new product category)", "that provides (key problem-solving capability)" and "Unlike (the product alternative)", and the whole product model, drawn as four rings: the generic, expected, augmented and potential product. Both are written for a market, not a deal.

Jonah Berger's corroborating evidence. Berger's free worksheet for his book The Catalyst asks whether the change you want is a "pebble" that one voice can move or a "boulder" that needs several, which similar but independent sources you can call on, and how to have people hear from them close together in time.

No published source shows a before-and-after business case for a cautious committee, or says which proofs to stack and when.

Where it has been tested.

In B2B sales

Nobody has measured the rewrite. No study compares deals where the seller rebuilt the case for the committee with deals where the seller repeated the case that won the champion, and nobody has put a reference from the buyer's own industry against one from another.

References have been measured in general. Harri Terho and Anne Jalkala linked the use of customer references to firms' selling performance in Industrial Marketing Management in 2017. V. Kumar, J. Andrew Petersen and Robert Leone measured the value of business references in the Journal of Marketing in 2013, with the fit between the client giving the reference and the prospect hearing it as one of four drivers. The open abstracts give neither the size nor the direction of that fit effect.

More sources are not the same as more confidence. Gartner reported in September 2019 that 89% of customers meet high-quality information during a purchase, that those who struggle to make sense of it are more likely to settle for a smaller, less disruptive purchase, and that reps who helped them make sense of it closed a high-quality, low-regret deal 80% of the time. No sample size is published, and Gartner sells the advice built on it. Stacking proof helps only if you also reconcile what the sources say.

In other disciplines

Independence does the work, not the number of voices. Stephen Harkins and Richard Petty gave students the same three arguments for a campus proposal in 1987 and changed one line. Telling them the three writers had formed a committee, rather than worked independently, wiped out the advantage of having three sources, unless the committee members were described as holding different views. With weak arguments, several sources did worse than one.

Same situation, not same category. Noah Goldstein, Robert Cialdini and Vladas Griskevicius tested cards in 1,595 hotel rooms in 2008. A card saying most "guests who stayed in this room" reused their towels produced 49.3% reuse, against 37.2% for a standard environmental appeal, while cards naming groups the guest belonged to, fellow citizens or men and women, did no better than a card about guests in general. A later replication did not find the same-room advantage, as set out under Find the bright spot inside the account.

Persuasion fades within a week. Alan Gerber and colleagues randomised about $2 million of television and radio advertising in the 2006 Texas governor's race and found strong but short-lived effects. J-PAL's summary puts the heaviest advertising at about 6 points of gain in the week it ran and about zero the week after.

But the one head-to-head test went against cramming it into one moment. A 1999 trial randomised 130 family members of drinkers who refused treatment to CRAFT, which coaches the family over several sessions, the Johnson intervention, a single confrontation meeting, or Al-Anon. Within six months 64%, 30% and 13% of the drinkers entered treatment, as Robert Meyers, Jane Ellen Smith and Denise Lash report; few families went through with the confrontation at all.

Caveat.

Nothing here tests a buying committee: the evidence is hotel guests, students, voters and families, and Moore's argument about visionaries and pragmatists has never been tested on a deal. The one experiment that compared a concentrated confrontation with a slower approach found the slower one worked better.

Takeaway.

When a deal your champion won stalls at the committee, stop repeating the champion's case. Rebuild it around a company in their industry already running this and around everything that has to be in place before it works, and bring proof from sources that are clearly independent of each other, close enough together that the first has not faded. Help the committee reconcile what those sources say. The reasoning is Moore's and has never been tested on a deal.

B2B sales research and data

  • Terho, H. and Jalkala, A. · Customer reference marketing: conceptualization, measurement and link to selling performance · 2017 · paper
    The authors linked the use of customer references to firms' selling performance in business markets. It shows references matter in general, not references from the buyer's own industry, which is what the card asks for.
  • Kumar, V., Petersen, J. A. and Leone, R. P. · Defining, Measuring, and Managing Business Reference Value · 2013 · paper
    The authors measured the value of business references and found the fit between the client giving a reference and the prospect hearing it to be one of four drivers. It bears on the card's same-industry rule, though the open abstract gives neither the size nor the direction of that effect.
  • Gartner vendor · Gartner Says the Biggest Challenge in B2B Sales Today Is a Lack of Customer Confidence · 2019 · vendor data
    Gartner reported that buyers who struggle to make sense of the information they meet are more likely to settle for a smaller purchase, and that reps who helped them make sense of it closed better deals. It is why the card asks you to reconcile the proof, not just stack it, from a company that sells this advice.

From other disciplines

  • Stephen G. Harkins and Richard E. Petty social psychology · Information Utility and the Multiple Source Effect · 1987 · paper
    Three arguments from three writers persuaded students more than one source did, unless they heard that the writers had worked as a committee, and with weak arguments several sources did worse than one. It is why the card asks for proof from sources that are clearly independent of each other.
  • Noah J Goldstein, Robert B Cialdini and Vladas Griskevicius consumer psychology · A Room with a Viewpoint: Using Social Norms to Motivate Environmental Conservation in Hotels · 2008 · paper
    In hotel field experiments, a card about guests who had stayed in the same room got more towels reused than cards about groups the guest belonged to. It supports proof from people in the same situation, not just the same category, though a replication did not find the same-room advantage.
  • Alan S. Gerber, James G. Gimpel, Donald P. Green and Daron R. Shaw political science · How Large and Long-Lasting Are the Persuasive Effects of Televised Campaign Ads? Results from a Randomized Field Experiment · 2011 · paper
    In a randomised field experiment, television adverts in a governor's race had strong effects on voters that faded fast. It is why the card brings the proof close together in time, not a reference call a month.
    Only the abstract is free to read.
  • Abdul Latif Jameel Poverty Action Lab political science · The Persuasive Effects of Televised Campaign Ads (evaluation summary) · 2011 · post
    J-PAL's summary puts figures on the same experiment: a clear gain in the week the heaviest advertising ran, and about none the week after. It is the reason the card says persuasion fades fast once it stops arriving.

Who says do not

  • Robert J. Meyers, Jane Ellen Smith and Denise N. Lash clinical psychology · A Program for Engaging Treatment-Refusing Substance Abusers into Treatment: CRAFT · 2005 · paper
    In a trial with families of drinkers who refused treatment, coaching over several sessions got far more drinkers into treatment than a single confrontation meeting. It is the one head-to-head test of concentrating everything into one moment, and it went against it.
