Stall: stuck in the machinery

Pre-answer the security questionnaire

Answer the standard security questionnaires before a buyer sends one, publish them on a tiered trust page, and add your AI answers now. Nobody has measured how much time it saves.

Evidence: documented practice. Sellers and trainers publish how they do it. No study has tested it.

What it is.

The buyer said yes, and then their security team sent a questionnaire with a few hundred questions. Your engineers will get to it when they can.

Answer the standard security questionnaires before any buyer sends one. Take the questionnaires the market actually uses, write your answers once, have them checked by whoever runs security, and keep them in a library your team can paste from. Put the evidence next to them on a trust page: the SOC 2 report, the penetration test, the data flow diagram, the list of subprocessors.

Then tier the page. Some material can sit in the open for anyone to read. Some opens after a request or an NDA. The buyer's analyst finds most of what they need without asking you, and the questionnaire that does arrive becomes mostly a copy job instead of a hunt across your engineering team.

What it looks like.

Adversis's answer bank. It runs through ten domains of the Shared Assessments SIG questionnaire, from security governance to business continuity, and gives every question two sample answers: one for an early-stage company that has just finished SOC 2, and one for a growth-stage company with a dedicated security team. The early-stage answer on security leadership names the person who leads security, their role and roughly how much of their time goes to it. Adversis tells you to adapt the answers, not copy them, because template language draws follow-up questions designed to find out whether the answer is real.

The same for AI. A second bank covers seven AI categories, from AI governance and prompt injection to agent permissions and AI-specific compliance. A companion post lists the AI questions buyers keep repeating, starting with "Do you train on customer data?", and the documents to have written before they arrive: the data flow diagram, the subprocessor list naming the specific models, how long embeddings are kept and how they are deleted, and an incident response plan for AI.

The questionnaire itself, free. The Cloud Security Alliance's CAIQ v4 is a spreadsheet of 261 questions, answered yes, no or not applicable, with space to justify each. Version 4 added a required column where you say who owns each control: you, the customer, a third party, or a split between them. A completed CAIQ can be published in the STAR Registry as a self-assessment, the free, public version of pre-answering.

Drata's own trust centre shows the tiering. Documents are filtered as public or private, with a button to request access to the restricted ones. The public side holds the external penetration test and the certifications. Other sections cover reports, including the CAIQ and data flow diagrams, product security, AI, legal documents with the subprocessor list, infrastructure and policies. Drata sells trust centre software, so the page is also a product demonstration.

Add the AI answers now. In 2026 the EU agreed to push back its rules for high-risk AI systems, to December 2027 for most of them and August 2028 for AI built into regulated products, as Gibson Dunn summarised the agreement in May 2026. The questions in a buyer's security review did not move: by Adversis's account, the AI questions above are already in them. Put your AI answers on the trust page: which model providers you use, whether you train on customer data, and how a customer opts out.

Where it has been tested.

In B2B sales

Nobody has measured it. No study compares deals where the seller pre-answered and published with deals where the seller waited for the questionnaire.

The one survey with numbers comes from a company that sells the answer. Whistic's 2023 State of Vendor Security report surveyed 524 security professionals at manager level or above. Buyers said 85% of vendor responses needed clarification, adding 4.4 days on average, and Whistic puts the average wait for a complete response at 17 days. 68% of buyers said they would probably still ask for more documents after receiving a SOC 2 or ISO 27001 report, so the audit report is not the whole answer. 79% of vendors said they would publish their security documents if they could control who saw them, the case for tiering. On the vendor side, 54% answered more than eleven questionnaires a month, and 64% said a salesperson almost always got pulled into answering. Whistic sells the catalogue these answers get published to, and its headline claim that publishing saves 17 days assumes the whole wait disappears. It is arithmetic on its own survey, not a measured comparison.

The advocate's own limit. Adversis says AI questionnaire tools handle about 70% of a questionnaire and stall on the rest: whether you have a dedicated security chief, your security monitoring setup, when you last rehearsed an incident, a current network diagram, and controls you have not built yet. The 70% is their estimate from practice, with no data published.

In other disciplines

Filling in the form for someone was tested properly once, on a government form. Eric Bettinger, Bridget Terry Long, Philip Oreopoulos and Lisa Sanbonmatsu ran a randomised experiment across 156 H&R Block tax offices in Ohio and Charlotte from 2008, published in 2012. For one group, software filled in about two thirds of the student aid form from their tax return, and a tax professional asked the rest in under ten minutes. A second group got personalised estimates of the aid they could receive and no help with the form. A third got nothing. Among independent adults with no prior college, aid applications rose from 16.1% in the group that got nothing to 42.8% in the pre-filled group. Among dependent students, college enrolment rose from 34.2% to 42.3%. The information-only group did no better than the group that got nothing. Handing people the numbers changed nothing. Handing them a form two thirds answered changed a great deal.

Caveat.

The only sales figures come from a survey and platform data owned by a company that sells the place you publish the answers, and they measure how long reviews take, not whether pre-answering shortens them. The student aid experiment tested low-income American tax filers facing one government form, not a security analyst paid to probe the answers. Nobody has compared the same kind of deal with and without a published answer set.

Takeaway.

Fill in a standard questionnaire such as the CAIQ before anyone asks, publish it where a buyer's analyst will find it, and put the evidence behind a tier they can open themselves. Write the hard answers in your own operational detail, because template wording draws more questions, and an audit report alone does not stop them. Add the AI answers now. The measured case that a pre-filled form beats information comes from outside selling. Inside selling, the numbers belong to the vendors.
