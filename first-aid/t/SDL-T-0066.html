Stall: stuck in the machinery

Speak finance's metric

Ask finance which number it decides on, payback, net present value or another, and which budget has room. Present in that number only, and never split invoices to slip under a limit.

Evidence: studies from another field. Studies in other fields, such as psychology or negotiation, back it. The library applies them to sales.

What it is.

Your business case shows a five-year net present value. The buyer's finance team decides on payback in months, and nobody told you.

Before you build the case, ask finance which number they actually decide on, and which budget line the money would come from. Some companies rank a purchase by net present value, some by internal rate of return against a hurdle rate, some by return on invested capital, and many decide on payback: how many months until it has paid for itself. Then present the purchase in that one number. If the buyer counts months to payback, a discounted cash flow model is the wrong document however good it is.

The other half of the move is restraint. Once you know where the approval limits sit and when the committee meets, work inside them. Do not split the purchase into smaller invoices to slip under a limit. That is exactly what the buyer's own rules forbid and exactly the pattern their auditors look for.

What it looks like.

No sales source publishes the question. The nearest published thing is the list of answers it can come back with.

The question in the 2022 Duke survey of finance chiefs. John Graham asked how often each firm uses a list of ten techniques when deciding which projects or acquisitions to pursue, scored from never to always. The list includes net present value, internal rate of return and hurdle rate, scenario analysis, payback, return on invested capital and profitability index. Graham and Campbell Harvey's 1999 survey asked the same question with a slightly different list. That list is the set of answers a buyer can give you.

The published rule against splitting. The US Federal Acquisition Regulation, at 13.003(c)(2), forbids breaking a requirement into several purchases below a threshold just to use simpler procedures or to avoid a rule that applies above it. The thresholds it protects are defined at 2.101: currently $15,000 for micro-purchases and $350,000 for the simplified procedure.

An approval matrix with turnaround times. Tallyfy, which sells workflow software, publishes an illustrative mid-market matrix in six bands, each with an approver and a turnaround. It runs from up to $1,000, approved by a department manager the same day, through $25,001 to $100,000, a VP in three to five business days, to over $500,000, the CEO and the board in ten to thirty days. The turnaround column is the one sellers forget. Each band costs calendar time as well as a signature.

Where it has been tested.

In B2B sales

Nobody has measured the move. No study compares sellers who asked finance which number it uses and presented in it with sellers who did not.

The nearest sales study is broader. Harri Terho, Andreas Eggert, Alexander Haas and Wolfgang Ulaga surveyed 816 salespeople and sales directors in 30 sales organisations for a 2015 paper in Industrial Marketing Management. Value-based selling, which includes showing customers the financial impact of working with you, went with better sales performance, a modest but reliable link. The salespeople rated both their own selling and their own performance, and the study is about translating benefits into money at all, not about matching the number finance has already chosen.

MEDDICC tells sellers the opposite. Its page on the economic buyer, from May 2026, tells sellers not to focus qualification on budget roles or processes, and separates the budget holder from the economic buyer, who can reach funds that are not tied to any budget. It is method with no measurement behind it, and it argues against the half of this move that maps budgets and approvals.

In other disciplines

Finance really does use different numbers. In the 2022 Duke survey, among 376 small firms with revenue under $1 billion, 66.4% always or almost always used payback, against 40.2% for net present value and 39.7% for internal rate of return. Among 85 large firms the order reversed: 77.4% net present value, 75.0% internal rate of return, 63.9% payback. The 1999 survey of 392 finance chiefs had already found that small firms used payback almost as often as the textbook methods. Both papers warn that the answers record what finance chiefs believe they do, which nobody checked against what they did.

Money moves when budgets are about to expire. Jeffrey Liebman and Neale Mahoney studied US federal procurement and found that spending in the last week of the fiscal year runs 4.9 times the average week, and that IT projects bought at year end get noticeably worse quality ratings. Those budgets expire by law, the strongest version of a pot that has room and is about to lose it.

What splitting under a threshold looks like from outside. Ján Palguta and Filip Pertold studied a Czech reform that let public bodies pick contractors freely below new thresholds. Contract values bunched just below the thresholds, and among those contracts the chance of the work going to a firm that could hide its owners tripled.

Caveat.

The finance surveys describe how companies say they rank their own projects, not how a buying committee judges a supplier's proposal. The threshold and year-end findings come from US and Czech public procurement, where budgets expire by law and the rules are published. So the safe claim is that the number varies, not that switching to it changes the answer.

Takeaway.

Ask which number finance decides on and which budget line the money comes from, then rebuild the case in that number and time it to a budget that still has room. The evidence shows the number really differs, sharply by company size, and that money moves when budgets are about to expire. It does not show that speaking finance's language wins deals. The only thing measured about thresholds is that bunching under them is visible to anyone who looks.
