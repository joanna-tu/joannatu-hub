Stall: cannot get it through the organisation

Map who listens to whom, then work back from the person who signs

Work out who listens to whom, mark each person for, against, undecided or unknown, then plan your conversations backwards from the person who signs and hold them one at a time before the meeting.

Evidence: documented practice. Sellers and trainers publish how they do it. No study has tested it.

What it is.

A decision meeting is coming, and you do not know who will say what in the room.

Before it happens, work out who listens to whom inside the buying organisation, then plan your conversations backwards from the person whose signature you need. Ask which agreements would have to be in place before that person says yes, then which agreements would have to come before those, until you reach a conversation you can have this week. Hold those conversations one at a time and in private. By the time the formal meeting happens, everyone in it has seen the proposal, said what they think, and had their objection answered or written into the document. The meeting only confirms a decision already made.

To decide where your time goes, mark each person on the map as for, against, undecided or unknown, and rank how much power they have. Most of your effort belongs with the undecided people who matter most.

What it looks like.

James Sebenius's backward mapping. In a 2004 Harvard Business School Working Knowledge piece, Sebenius sets out five steps. Map the parties already involved and those who might get involved, with their interests and their options if there is no deal. Estimate how hard each one is to win and what having them on board is worth. Identify the relationships: "who influences whom, who tends to defer to whom", and who owes whom a favour. Then take the hardest person to persuade and ask which prior agreements among which of the others "would maximize the chances of the target saying yes on your terms", and keep asking that question backwards. His worked case is Steve Perlman, who signed Sony and Philips before going to the investors, not after, and used those agreements to raise his valuation at WebTV. The method is the setup half of 3-D Negotiation, written with David Lax.

Nemawashi, the Toyota version. Art Smalley's entry in the Art of Lean encyclopedia gives six steps: draft a proposal, identify every stakeholder, visit each one individually, listen and revise, go back to those whose input changed it, and only then present it formally. On the visits: "Not by email. Not by group presentation. One-on-one, face to face." A proposal presented without that groundwork, he writes, is typically sent back, "Not because the idea is wrong, but because the process was wrong." Rochelle Kopp of Japan Intercultural Consulting adds the reason senior people come first: someone who hears about a proposal for the first time in the meeting feels ignored, and may reject it for that reason alone.

The power map. Lisa VeneKlasen and Valerie Miller's advocacy guide has you list each institution and the person inside it, mark them O for opposed, U for uncommitted, S for supporter or ? for don't know, then rank their power from 1, the most powerful, to 4. The questions that follow: who are your immediate allies, who do you need to persuade, who are your strongest opponents.

Who makes the ask, from fundraising. David Dunlop's approach to major gifts, built with Buck Smith at Cornell and published in a 1993 book chapter, moves a donor step by step towards a gift. Ellucian's white paper, from a company that sells fundraising software, quotes its rule for the last step: decide who makes the ask, usually the person with the best relationship, often a peer, "a person to whom the donor just can't say no."

No sales methodology publishes the map itself.

Where it has been tested.

In B2B sales

One vendor number on order, none on the map. The only measurement of sequence in selling is Gong's finding that deals starting with an executive won slightly less often than deals where the executive arrived around the third meeting, set out under Build the champion first, widen with them. Gong sells the software behind it and the deals were never assigned an order. Nobody has compared sellers who mapped who defers to whom and worked back from the person who signs with sellers who met the same people in whatever order the calendar produced.

In other disciplines

Managers who got an issue onto the agenda. Jane Dutton, Susan Ashford, Regina O'Neill and Katherine Lawrence interviewed 42 middle managers at one American hospital about 82 attempts to get top management's attention, published in the Academy of Management Journal in 2001. More than 60% mentioned involving someone more senior and 48% involving peers, and accounts of bringing in their own boss came almost entirely from the attempts that worked, which the authors describe as "an incremental, cascading approach to selling an issue upward." These are the managers' own accounts after the fact, with no comparison of one order against another.

Lobbyists work their allies, and avoid their opponents. Richard Hall and Alan Deardorff start their 2006 paper in the American Political Science Review from a finding repeated across lobbying research: "lobbyists concentrate on their allies, avoid their enemies, and lobby undecideds infrequently." They model lobbying as giving allies information and help rather than persuading opponents. That describes what professionals do, not whether it pays, and it cuts against spending your time converting a blocker.

The methods themselves are practice. Nobody has compared nemawashi, backward mapping, the power map or the fundraising ladder with doing without them. What exists is accumulated practice written down by the people who teach it. The evidence on which individual to find first, the person colleagues check with, is under Find the person people check with.

Caveat.

The hospital study relies on managers explaining their own successes long after the event, and the lobbying work describes what professionals do rather than what works, so neither compares one order of approach with another. The only number attached to order anywhere comes from a vendor's own data on deals that differed in many ways besides when the executive appeared.

Takeaway.

Before you book anything, write down who defers to whom, mark each person for, against, undecided or unknown, and start at the signature, working back until you reach a conversation you can have this week. Spend your time on the undecided people with power and on your allies, not on converting the blocker in the meeting. Treat the map as a way to find the conversation you have been avoiding; nobody has tested it against the alternative.
