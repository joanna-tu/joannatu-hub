Executive to executive, through your contact

Pair one of your executives with one of theirs, matched on title and introduced through your champion, while the deal is still healthy. In Gong's data, evaluations that started with an executive won less often.

Evidence: documented practice. Sellers and trainers publish how they do it. No study has tested it.

What it is.

The deal is going well. In two months it will need a signature from someone you have never met.

Put one of your executives in front of one of theirs now, matched on title or function, and route the introduction through someone already inside the deal. You brief your executive on the account and draft the note they will send. Then you leave the relationship alone. When the deal later jams in procurement or on a signature, the two executives already know each other, and one message can move it.

The timing is half the move. Make the introduction while the evaluation is going well. An executive who appears for the first time during a rescue looks like one more vendor resource sent to save a quarter.

What it looks like.

Alex Buckles's two emails at Forecastable. The first goes from the rep to his own CEO, headed "Executive Alignment Request". It gives the background: the deal size, the account, the close date, the stage, the hesitation he senses from the buyer's COO, and the former employer the CEO and the COO share, with the years they overlapped. Then it asks the CEO to send a note, with the goal of building a relationship and drawing out the concern the COO is not voicing. The second email is that note, drafted for the CEO. It opens on the shared former employer, not the deal, with Buckles's instruction to the reader: "Don't make it all about THIS deal. Insert something personal." It says the two teams are starting to work together, offers help, asks for 30 minutes in the next couple of weeks and gives a mobile number. His pairing rule: match titles or functions. A VP of sales will not work against a technical VP.

Nick Cegelski's briefing checklist at 30MPC. Ten headings for briefing your own executive before the meeting: an overview of the account and competition; who you have met, with title, tenure and influence; who you still need to meet; advocates and past users; their current tools and renewal dates; their revenue and anything public from annual reports or earnings calls; how the sale has gone so far and why this meeting is happening; the business problems you solve at each level; their growth plans; their hesitations; and the role you want the executive to play, including what you do not want them to do.

Where it has been tested.

In B2B sales

The only timing numbers come from one vendor's data. Gong, 30MPC and Jen Allen-Knuth analysed more than a million sales cycles involving executives, published on Gong's blog in February 2026 and in Armand Farrokh's 30MPC newsletter the day before. Farrokh reports win rates about 1.5 times higher in mid-market, enterprise and strategic deals when an executive was directly involved. Both report that win rates fell about 6% when an evaluation started with an executive and rose about 5% when executives came in around the third touchpoint. Gong sells the software that recorded these deals and publishes no method. The comparison is between deals that happened to bring executives in at different points, and it says nothing about who made the introduction or whether the executives were matched.

The nearest academic study is about accounts, not stalled deals. Nektarios Tzempelikos interviewed 304 suppliers for a 2015 paper in the Journal of Business and Industrial Marketing. Suppliers whose top managers were committed to a key account got them more involved, involvement went with a better relationship, and a better relationship went with better financial results. Everything is the supplier's own report at one point in time, and none of it concerns matching executives or introductions through a champion.

The pairing rules have never been measured. No study compares matched executives with whoever is free, an introduction by the champion with one by the rep, or shared history with none. Buckles's warning that an unintroduced executive gets a low response is his own experience, with no numbers behind it.

In other disciplines

What a senior person's backing is worth once it becomes an order. Paul Nutt followed 356 decisions in medium and large organisations in the US and Canada for two years each, for a 1999 paper in the Academy of Management Executive. Edicts, where a manager announces the decision without consulting the people it affects, appeared in 40% of cases and did worst: 53% were still in place two years later and only 35% were fully used. Where the manager first worked with the key people to agree the need, which happened in only 7% of cases, 96% of decisions were still in place and 92% fully used. The executive is worth most doing that groundwork and least giving the order.

Caveat.

Nutt studied managers imposing decisions inside their own organisations, not an executive backing a supplier, so the transfer to a buyer's executive is an assumption. The Gong figures are correlations from the company whose software recorded the deals, and they record when an executive appeared, not how the introduction was made. Nobody has compared the same kind of deal with and without a matched, champion-introduced pairing.

Takeaway.

Make the introduction while the deal is healthy, brief your executive against the ten headings, and have the champion or your executive send the note instead of the rep announcing a new face. The one measured figure that cuts against the usual advice is that evaluations starting with an executive won less often. Early is not the same as first.
